# Measuring a Child's Bargaining or Decision Weight in U.S. Families

## Supervisor research brief — 12 September 2026

## Bottom line

There is **no variable in the PSID or its Child Development Supplement (CDS) that directly equals a child's structural bargaining weight or Pareto weight**. A Pareto weight is a latent model object: it is the coefficient placed on one household member's cardinal utility after utility functions have been normalized. Survey answers such as “the child has a lot of choice” measure **reported decision authority or autonomy**, not that coefficient.

The PSID/CDS nevertheless contains unusually relevant proxies. The closest are the Primary Caregiver–Child items `E43–E46`, available in 2014, 2019, 2021, and 2024:

| Domain | 2014 | 2019 | 2021 | 2024 | Response |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Choice over room decorations | `P14E43` | `P19E43` | `P21E43` | `P24E43` | Very little / some / a lot |
| Choice over clothes | `P14E44` | `P19E44` | `P21E44` | `P24E44` | Very little / some / a lot |
| Choice over recreational pursuits, hobbies, or leisure activities | `P14E45` | `P19E45` | `P21E45` | `P24E45` | Very little / some / a lot |
| Choice over managing own homework | `P14E46` | `P19E46` | `P21E46` | `P24E46` | Very little / some / a lot |

For this thesis, `E45` and `E46` are the most defensible: they refer directly to the leisure–study allocation over which the parent and child bargain in the model. The CDS team describes `E43–E46` as measures of the amount of choice children are permitted in their activities and home environment, developed for the HOME scale—not as measures of bargaining power.[^1]

My recommended language to the supervisor is:

> “I do not observe the child's Pareto weight. I observe domain-specific indicators of child autonomy. I can use these indicators as additional moments or through an ordinal measurement equation to discipline the age profile of the latent weight, subject to an explicit mapping between reported autonomy and the structural object.”

The best empirical plan is **not to replace PSID with another dataset immediately**. Keep PSID/CDS for the main structural analysis, use CDS `E45` and `E46` as the closest within-dataset measures, and use the NLSY79 Child Supplement as an external U.S. validation source because it asks children themselves who makes decisions in seven domains.

## 1. Four concepts that must not be treated as synonyms

### 1.1 Decision authority or autonomy

This is an observed answer about who decides, or how much choice the child has, in a specified domain. Examples include clothes, friends, spending money, leisure activities, homework, television, or curfew.

It is usually:

- domain-specific;
- ordinal or categorical;
- reported by either the child or caregiver;
- the equilibrium outcome of both parental permission and the child's attempt to exercise control.

This is what CDS, NLSY79 Child, NLSY97, FFCWS, and Add Health most directly measure.

### 1.2 Resource share

This is the fraction of household resources allocated to a child. It may be inferred from private assignable goods such as child clothing, or from a structural demand system. A resource share is useful for child welfare and poverty analysis, but it is **not a bargaining weight**. Loving parents may allocate large resources to a child who has no decision authority.

### 1.3 Pareto or welfare weight

In a collective household model,

$$
\max_x \; \mu_t U^P(x) + (1-\mu_t)U^C(x),
$$

the weight summarizes where the household locates on its Pareto frontier. It is generally unobserved; only choices generated by the preferences, constraints, technology, and weight are observed. Dauphin et al. state this directly: structural demands and Pareto weights are unobservable, while reduced-form household demands are observed.[^2]

A numerical Pareto weight also depends on the cardinal normalization of each utility function. Multiplying the child's utility by a positive constant and compensating by changing the weight can leave behavior unchanged. Therefore, “the child's true bargaining weight is 0.35” is not meaningful without fixing utility scales and the full structural model.

### 1.4 Outside option or distribution factor

An outside option is what the child can obtain if cooperation breaks down; a distribution factor is a variable that shifts bargaining power without directly changing preferences or the budget set. Potential wages, legally controlled income, transfers paid directly to a member, custody rules, or opportunities to live elsewhere can sometimes serve this role. For children, credible outside options are weak and ethically/institutionally constrained, which is one reason direct identification is difficult.

### Key implication

The data can show that a child participates in decisions without identifying a unique cardinal Pareto weight. Conversely, a child may have a positive modeled welfare weight because parents care about the child's utility even when the child does not literally negotiate.

## 2. What PSID/CDS actually contains

### 2.1 The closest variables: `E43–E46`

The official 2014 codebook defines the four choice items and the 2014 user guide explains their purpose.[^1][^3] They persist in the 2019, 2021, and 2024 Primary Caregiver–Child files.[^4][^5][^6]

The eligible ages are not identical across domains and waves:

| Item | 2014 age universe | 2019/2021/2024 age universe | Relevance to this model |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `E43`, room decoration | 9–17 | 6–18 | General personal/environmental autonomy |
| `E44`, clothing | 9–17 | 6–18 | Personal consumption/taste autonomy |
| `E45`, leisure activities | 9–17 | 9–18 | **Directly related to child leisure** |
| `E46`, managing homework | 12–17 | 12–18 | **Directly related to study choice** |

Important harmonization details:

- The 2014 `E45` wording is “recreational pursuits”; later waves say “hobbies or other leisure activities done for enjoyment.” This is close but should be tested for a wave discontinuity.
- `E46` has the narrowest universe. A common-domain index containing all four items should therefore normally use ages 12–17 when pooling all waves.
- `E42`, choice over breakfast and lunch foods, is for ages 1–5 and has four response categories rather than three. It should **not** be quietly inserted into the adolescent `E43–E46` scale.
- These are caregiver reports (“How much choice do you give…”), not the child's report. They measure granted or perceived autonomy from the caregiver's side.
- Codes `8` and `9` are “don't know” and “not ascertained/refused”; `0` means inapplicable because of the age/grade universe. Zero is not “no autonomy.”

### 2.2 Rule-setting variables

The CDS Primary Caregiver–Child files also ask about rules. In 2019, examples include:

- `P19C5`: bedtime;
- `P19C6`: homework;
- `P19C9`: after-school activities;
- `P19C10`: friends and places the child may go;
- `P19C11`: dating;
- `P19C12`: curfew;
- `P19C13`: hours of work.

The useful response structure is:

1. clear rules that are enforced;
2. general rules that are monitored;
3. rules exist, but the child makes their own choices;
4. no rules;
5. additional volunteered or inapplicable categories, depending on the item.

For example, the official codebook gives this structure for bedtime and homework.[^7] Category 3 is a clean autonomy indicator. Category 4 is **ambiguous**: no rules may mean mature independence, but it may also mean low parental monitoring or disengagement. It should not automatically be scored as more autonomy than category 3.

These rules variables can complement `E45–E46`, particularly around the model's age-six voice threshold, but their universes and wording must be checked item-by-item and wave-by-wave.

### 2.3 Child-controlled or child-directed resources

The CDS also records allowance and assets held for the child. In 2019, for example:

- `P19G1`: receives an allowance;
- `P19G2` and `P19G2A`: amount and periodicity;
- `P19G3–G5`: whether allowance depends on chores, family rules, or schoolwork;
- `P19G6_*`: who pays the allowance;
- `P19G7–G10`: stocks, funds, trusts, checking, or savings for the child.[^8]

These measure resources, incentives, or parental investment. They do not establish that the child controls those assets, and therefore are not direct bargaining measures. They are potentially useful covariates or mechanisms.

### 2.4 Why the PSID Core does not solve the problem

The main PSID family interview is rich in income, labor supply, wealth, transfers, and family structure, but it generally identifies the family respondent's account of household outcomes. It does not provide a child-level variable called a bargaining weight. The CDS is the relevant supplement, and PSID's own documentation routes users to the CDS Primary Caregiver, Child, and time-diary files.[^9]

### 2.5 What can and cannot be claimed using CDS

Defensible claims:

- “The caregiver reports how much choice the child is given in particular domains.”
- “Reported choice tends to vary by age, domain, family structure, and child characteristics.”
- “Homework and leisure choice provide measurement information about latent autonomy.”

Claims to avoid:

- “`P19E46=3` means the child has a Pareto weight of 0.5.”
- “No household rules means maximum bargaining power.”
- “A single averaged autonomy score is automatically a structural bargaining weight.”
- “The CDS observes a negotiated household allocation.” The item wording is about permission granted by a caregiver.

## 3. NLSY79 Child Supplement: the strongest direct U.S. alternative

### 3.1 Study design

The NLSY79 Child and Young Adult study follows the biological children of women in the NLSY79. More than 10,000 children had participated by 2018.[^10] Its Child Self-Administered Supplement (CSAS) was administered repeatedly from 1988 through 2014, generally to children ages 10–14, and covers family decision-making, parent-child interaction, work, money, school, peers, and behavior.[^11]

This design has three major strengths for the thesis:

1. the **child reports** decision authority;
2. the same child can appear in multiple biennial rounds while age-eligible;
3. biological siblings can be linked through the NLSY79 mother, permitting mother/sibling fixed effects.

It is not a fresh cross-section of all U.S. children in each year. The children are descendants of the original female NLSY79 cohort, so analysts must use appropriate child weights, account for clustering by mother, and be explicit about the target population.

### 3.2 Exact decision-making block

The 2002 CAPI instrument names the seven items `CSAS010A–CSAS010G` and asks who usually makes decisions about:

| Question | Domain |
|---|---|
| `CSAS010A` | buying the child's clothes |
| `CSAS010B` | how the child spends their money |
| `CSAS010C` | which friends the child goes out with |
| `CSAS010D` | how late the child can stay out |
| `CSAS010E` | how much allowance the child receives |
| `CSAS010F` | how much television the child can watch |
| `CSAS010G` | the child's religious training |

Respondents can choose **all people who help decide**: the child, mother, father, stepfather, stepmother, grandparents, friends, someone else, or no one; allowance has a “does not receive allowance” option.[^12] The 2000 paper form used the same seven domains with a slightly shorter decision-maker list.[^13] Earlier waves use round-specific reference numbers, so extraction should be based on question text and survey year in NLS Investigator rather than assuming the 2002 question names apply unchanged to every wave.

This block permits three conceptually distinct outcomes in each domain:

- **sole child decision**: child selected and no adult selected;
- **shared decision**: child and at least one parent/caregiver selected;
- **adult-only decision**: parent/caregiver selected and child not selected.

Do not force these into a single linear ordering without testing it. Lundberg, Romich, and Tsang find that shared and sole decisions have different predictors; shared decision-making may be a deliberate parental investment in the child's decision skills, not merely a midpoint between parental control and child independence.[^14]

### 3.3 Additional NLSY79 measures that improve interpretation

The CSAS contains a second family-rules block:

- `CSAS004A–D`: whether rules exist for television, informing parents of whereabouts, homework, and dating/parties;
- `CSAS005A–D`: how much say the child has in making each rule—none, a little, some, or a lot;
- `CSAS006A–D`: how often child and parents argue about each rule;
- `CSAS007*`: how often a parent talks over important decisions with the child;
- `CSAS008*`: how often the parent listens to the child's side of an argument;
- `CSAS009*`: how often the parent knows who the child is with when away from home;
- `CSAS020C`: how much the child tells parents about spending money.

The rule and “say” items were adapted from the U.S. National Survey of Children and were asked repeatedly.[^15] These distinguish at least four mechanisms:

- formal decision authority;
- consultation and communication;
- conflict or resistance;
- monitoring and information.

That distinction is valuable because a noncooperative child can obtain de facto autonomy by resisting, hiding information, or violating rules even when parents do not grant authority.

### 3.4 How the main economics paper uses the data

Lundberg, Romich, and Tsang (2009) use NLSY79 Child observations from 1994–2000 and a noncooperative model of parental control and child resistance.[^14] Their empirical approach is:

1. code sole-child, shared, and parent-only decision indicators by domain;
2. sum them into ordinal count indices;
3. estimate ordered logit models;
4. estimate family fixed-effects ordered logits using siblings with the same mother;
5. separately analyze personal domains and human-capital-related domains.

They exclude allowance and curfew from the main **sole-decision** total because child-only reports are rare and potentially unreliable in those domains. Their descriptive evidence shows that sole authority is common for spending, friends, and television, but rare for allowance and curfew. Sole decision-making rises rapidly from ages 10 to 14, while shared decision-making rises mostly through about age 12 and then flattens.[^16]

This paper is the closest published empirical precedent for constructing a U.S. child decision-power measure from direct survey questions.

### 3.5 Best use of NLSY79 for this thesis

Use NLSY79 to estimate an **external age profile and domain pattern**, not to assign a cardinal value to the model's Pareto weight. Recommended outputs are:

- probability of sole child decision by age and domain;
- probability of shared decision by age and domain;
- probability the child has “some” or “a lot” of say over homework rules;
- separate profiles by sex, family structure, parental education, and child work/earnings;
- sibling fixed-effects estimates for child capability, age, and earnings where sample size permits.

For alignment with the structural model, prioritize:

- television/leisure and homework-rule say as proxies for study–leisure control;
- spending money as a resource-control proxy;
- allowance and child earnings as potential determinants of voice, not voice itself.

## 4. Other relevant U.S. datasets

### 4.1 Ranked comparison

| Rank | Dataset | Direct measure | Respondent and age | Main advantage | Main limitation |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | NLSY79 Child | Seven decision domains plus say in four household-rule domains | Child, mainly 10–14; repeated 1988–2014 | Richest direct child report; repeated observations; siblings | Older cohorts; child sample derives from NLSY79 women; limited to 10–14 for CSAS |
| 2 | PSID/CDS | Choice over room, clothes, leisure, homework; rule-setting | Caregiver; domain-specific ages 6/9/12–17/18; 2014–2024 | Same families and economic data as the thesis; recent waves | Caregiver report; no direct Pareto weight; changing age universes |
| 3 | NLSY97 | Who sets limits on curfew, television/movies, and friends | Youth and parent; younger birth cohorts in early rounds | Matched parent-youth perceptions; joint-decision category; created indices | Only three domains; mostly early survey rounds and one cohort |
| 4 | Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study (FFCWS), Year 15 | Parent decides / teen decides / jointly decide for curfew, media, and friends | Teen and primary caregiver, about age 15 | Matched reports and extremely rich childhood histories | One main decision snapshot; urban birth cohort with oversample of nonmarital births |
| 5 | Add Health, Waves I–II | Whether parents let youth decide in seven domains | Adolescent, grades 7–12 at baseline | Large national adolescent sample; seven clean items; broad outcomes | Binary yes/no; no joint category; not designed around household resources |
| 6 | National Survey of Children, 1976–1987 | Child say in rules, parent-child decision styles | Children 7–11 at baseline, 12–16 at Wave II | Historical precursor to NLSY items; child and parent reports | Small, old cohort; attrition and deliberate oversampling require weights |

### 4.2 NLSY97

In early rounds, NLSY97 youth report who sets limits for:

- how late they may stay out;
- television shows and movies;
- whom they may spend time with.

The response distinguishes parent-set, youth-set, and jointly set limits. Round 1 asked comparable questions of a responding parent. Child Trends created:

- `FP_YHLIMITS`: youth-reported limit-setting index, rounds 1–3;
- `FP_PHLIMITS`: parent-reported index, round 1;
- `FP_YHBROKED`: youth report of broken limits;
- `FP_PHBROKED`: parent report of broken limits.[^17]

The item-level parent variables begin at `R06893.00–R06895.00` in the round-1 Parent Questionnaire.[^18] NLSY97 is particularly good for measuring **disagreement between parent and child reports**, which can itself reveal information about conflict, hidden action, or differing perceptions of authority.

### 4.3 Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study (FFCWS)

At Year 15, the primary caregiver items `p6d18–p6d20` and teen items `k6c7a–k6c7c` ask who decides:

- how late the teen stays out;
- which television shows and movies the teen watches;
- whom the teen can spend time with.

Responses are caregiver/parent, youth, or joint decision.[^19] This is one of the cleanest U.S. parent-child paired modules. The study follows births in large U.S. cities around 2000 and intentionally contains many families with unmarried parents; estimates must use the proper weights and should not be described as a simple national sample of all children.[^20]

For this thesis, FFCWS is useful as a robustness dataset at age 15 and for studying whether early resources, family structure, parental relationship quality, or neighborhood conditions predict later teen authority.

### 4.4 Add Health

Wave I Section 16 contains seven child-reported binary variables `H1WP1–H1WP7`: whether parents let the adolescent make their own decisions about weekend curfew, friends, clothing, amount of television, television programs, weekday bedtime, and food.[^21] These are often summed into an autonomy-granting or reverse-coded parental-control index. Add Health began with a nationally representative school-based sample of U.S. adolescents in grades 7–12 in 1994–95 and followed them into adulthood.[^22]

Advantages:

- much larger adolescent sample than NLSY79 Child;
- strong coverage of behavior, peers, school, health, work, family relationships, and later outcomes;
- seven domains with identical yes/no wording.

Limitations:

- the binary format cannot distinguish sole from shared decision-making;
- it measures permission, not actual bargaining or allocation;
- the school-based baseline and historical cohort require care in defining the population;
- there is limited direct household consumption and wealth detail compared with PSID.

### 4.5 National Survey of Children (NSC)

The NSC followed a U.S. probability sample of 2,301 children ages 7–11 in 1976, with follow-ups in 1981 and 1987; 1,147 participated in the third wave.[^23] Wave II contains rules, how much say the child has in those rules, and parent-child decision/communication measures. It is the source for several NLSY79 CSAS items.[^15]

It is useful for historical comparison or replication, but the NLSY79 is normally preferable because it repeats and extends the same measurement in a larger, better-known longitudinal system.

### 4.6 Supporting U.S. datasets that do not directly measure bargaining

**Consumer Expenditure Survey (CE).** It can support resource-allocation work because some apparel records identify the recipient's age/sex category. However, current CE public-use documentation warns that purchasers or consumers generally cannot be identified in multi-person households.[^24] It is therefore not a clean substitute for the assignable-goods datasets used by Dunbar, Lewbel, and Pendakur.

**American Time Use Survey (ATUS).** Respondents age 15 or older provide a one-day diary, so 15–17-year-olds can reveal their study, work, household production, and leisure. It has no child bargaining question and contains only limited information for other household members.[^25] It can validate time allocation, not the weight.

**CDS time diaries.** These are preferable when maintaining the PSID sample because they directly measure children's activities and can be linked to the caregiver autonomy items. Again, time allocation is an outcome of preferences, productivity, constraints, and authority—not a standalone bargaining measure.[^9]

**ECLS, National Survey of Children's Health, and NSCAW.** These contain valuable parenting, development, service, or shared medical-decision measures, but they are not strong primary choices for a general within-family child bargaining weight. NSCAW is also specific to children investigated by Child Protective Services.

## 5. Methods used in economics papers

### 5.1 Direct reported decision authority

**Representative paper:** Lundberg, Romich, and Tsang (2009), *Decision-Making by Children*.[^14]

Observed outcomes are sole child, shared, or parent-only decisions. Econometric methods include:

- domain-level multinomial or ordered response models;
- count indices estimated with ordered logit or Poisson models;
- sibling/mother fixed effects;
- age profiles and interactions with resources or child capability.

What it identifies: reported equilibrium authority and its correlates.

What it does not identify: a cardinal Pareto weight, unless embedded in a separate structural mapping.

### 5.2 Revealed-preference tests for the number of decision-makers

**Representative paper:** Dauphin, El Lahga, Fortin, and Lacroix (2011), *Are Children Decision-Makers Within the Household?*[^2]

The paper uses U.K. Family Expenditure Survey households containing a couple and one co-resident child with positive earnings. It estimates a quadratic almost ideal demand system and tests rank restrictions implied by collective rationality. The number of linearly independent compensated price or distribution-factor effects gives a lower bound on the apparent number of decision-makers. Testing three potential decision-makers requires at least seven goods.[^26]

What it identifies: whether observed aggregate demand is consistent with more than the two parents influencing choices, under collective-model restrictions.

What it does not identify: a direct survey measure or necessarily the exact level of the child's weight. The test gives a minimal/apparent number of decision-makers and can lose power when weights are locally constant, preferences coincide, or distribution-factor effects are dependent.

### 5.3 Resource shares from private assignable goods

**Representative paper:** Dunbar, Lewbel, and Pendakur (2013), *Children's Resources in Collective Households*.[^27]

Using Malawi household expenditure data, the authors identify individual resource shares from Engel curves for a private assignable good—clothing. Core restrictions include:

- resource shares do not vary with total expenditure over the relevant range;
- limited similarity restrictions on individual preferences across people or household types;
- the good is private and its user is assignable.

What it identifies: the level of the share of total household consumption resources going to each member.

What it does not identify: the child's preference weight in a general bargaining model. Resource shares are often more stable welfare objects because they do not depend on a cardinal utility normalization.

### 5.4 Historical income contribution as bargaining leverage

**Representative paper:** Moehling (2005), *“She Has Suddenly Become Powerful”: Youth Employment and Household Decision Making in the Early Twentieth Century*.[^28]

Using the 1917–1919 U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Cost of Living Survey, the paper shows that working children received more clothing expenditure and that clothing spending rose with the income the child brought into the household. This is evidence consistent with child earnings increasing influence.

Identification warning: actual child earnings are jointly chosen with work and schooling. They can change the budget and the child's power simultaneously. Potential wages, policy-induced earnings changes, or child-directed transfers are more credible distribution factors than realized earnings alone.

### 5.5 Structural collective time-allocation models

**Recent example:** Muñoz-Alvarado, *Power to the Teens? A Model of Parents' and Teens' Collective Labor Supply*.[^29]

The paper combines Costa Rican household survey data with a conditional cash-transfer program, models parents and teens as potential collective decision-makers, estimates labor supply, schooling, domestic work, and home production, and tests unitary versus collective restrictions by likelihood-ratio tests. It also uses a control-function correction and imputes teen wages. This is close in spirit to the present thesis because bargaining affects the teen's time allocation rather than only consumption.

### 5.6 New evidence on children's decision status

Sözbir's *Children's Say in the Household*, published in the *Journal of Development Economics*, uses 2011/12–2018/19 Bangladesh household survey data for work-eligible children ages 12–17 and price-based collective-model restrictions.[^30] It rejects a unitary model and finds some evidence that children are decision-makers, while emphasizing the methodological difficulty of comparing working and nonworking children.

Although these last three applications are not modern U.S. household panels, they supply the structural and revealed-preference methods that can be combined with U.S. measurement data.

## 6. How this connects to the current thesis model

The implemented model places parent weight $\tilde\mu_t$ and child weight $1-\tilde\mu_t$ on utilities. The estimation memo uses

$$
\tilde\mu_t=1 \quad (t<6), \qquad
\tilde\mu_t=\mu_0+\mu_1(t-5) \quad (t\geq6),
$$

with $\mu_0=1$ and currently calibrated $\mu_1=-0.04$. Thus, the implied child weight is 0.04 at age 6 and 0.48 at age 17. The child bargains over own study time and leisure, and the human-capital technology also changes with age.[^31]

### 6.1 The present identification problem

The current SMM uses nine estimated parameters and ten behavioral moments: mean consumption and parental hours, plus early/late parental time, monetary investment, child study time, and child human capital. Both the welfare-weight slope $\mu_1$ and study-productivity slope $\sigma_{41}$ are fixed.[^31]

Study time alone does not cleanly identify $\mu_1$ because it also responds to:

- the child's leisure preference $\lambda_1$;
- the child's human-capital valuation $\lambda_2$;
- parental valuation $\phi_3$;
- study productivity $\sigma_{40}$ and $\sigma_{41}$;
- the continuation value of human capital;
- constraints and other endogenous choices.

This is exactly where an autonomy measurement can help: it is more closely related to the allocation of decision authority than the amount of study chosen.

### 6.2 The most defensible mapping

Let $Y_{idt}\in\{1,2,3\}$ be the caregiver's response for child $i$, domain $d$, and age $t$. Introduce latent autonomy:

$$
A^*_{idt}=a_d+b_d\,[1-\tilde\mu_t]+X_{it}'\gamma_d+u_i+\delta_{wave}+\varepsilon_{idt},
$$

and thresholds

$$
Y_{idt}=k \quad \text{if}\quad \kappa_{d,k-1}<A^*_{idt}\leq\kappa_{d,k}.
$$

Use an ordered logit or ordered probit likelihood, or match simulated response probabilities within SMM. Here:

- $d=E45$ captures leisure autonomy;
- $d=E46$ captures homework/study autonomy;
- $X$ can contain sex, family structure, parental education, income, number of siblings, and caregiver characteristics;
- $u_i$ captures stable child/family heterogeneity when repeated observations are available;
- wave effects absorb wording, mode, and period differences.

This does **not** become identified for free. The scale of $A^*$ requires a normalization, and $b_d$, the thresholds, and the scale of the Pareto weight can trade off. Joint estimation with the structural behavioral choices plus a stated normalization is necessary.

### 6.3 Three feasible empirical strategies

#### Strategy A — recommended first: external validation

1. Keep $\mu_1=-0.04$ calibrated.
2. Estimate weighted age profiles for CDS `E45` and `E46`.
3. Plot the model's implied child weight against standardized autonomy profiles.
4. Test whether the assumed monotonic increase and relative age gradient are consistent with the data.
5. Validate the shape externally using NLSY79 child reports.

Advantages: transparent, quick, does not pretend ordinal responses are cardinal weights.

Limitation: $\mu_1$ remains calibrated rather than estimated.

#### Strategy B — preferred thesis extension: add autonomy moments

1. Fix $\sigma_{41}$ at its current calibration.
2. Estimate $\mu_1$ jointly with the existing nine parameters.
3. Add several independent autonomy moments, such as the probability of “a lot” of leisure choice at ages 9–11, 12–14, and 15–17 and the same for homework at ages 12–14 and 15–17.
4. Use an explicit, parsimonious measurement link from $1-\tilde\mu_t$ to ordered response probabilities.
5. Compute and inspect the full moment Jacobian, profile objective in $\mu_1$, and report weak-identification diagnostics.

Advantages: directly adds information related to authority and gives more moments than parameters.

Limitations: the answer depends on the measurement link and its normalization. Adding many threshold/loading parameters can consume the extra identifying information.

#### Strategy C — ambitious: endogenize bargaining power

Specify $\mu_{it}$ as a function of distribution factors and outside options, for example:

$$
1-\tilde\mu_{it}=\Lambda(\alpha_t+Z_{it}'\pi),
$$

where $Z$ may include child potential wage, legally controlled earnings, policy eligibility, custody/institutional variables, or exogenous changes in opportunities. Estimate jointly from time allocation, investments, outcomes, and autonomy reports.

Advantages: connects “power” to an economic mechanism and allows heterogeneity.

Limitations: credible exclusion restrictions for U.S. minors are difficult; realized child work and earnings are endogenous; this is a major model expansion.

### 6.4 Recommended sample and moments

For a PSID/CDS implementation:

- Main common sample: child ages 12–17, living with two parents and no siblings if matching the model literally; alternatively retain broader families and explicitly model/control household composition.
- Primary measures: `E45` and `E46`, not a mechanically averaged four-item score.
- Secondary measures: `E43`, `E44`, and “rules but child makes own choices” in relevant `C` items.
- Wave strategy: pool 2014, 2019, 2021, and 2024 with wave fixed effects; test item-by-wave interactions.
- Outcome coding: retain ordinal categories and match category probabilities; do not treat 1, 2, 3 as equally spaced by default.
- Survey design: use CDS weights and cluster repeated children/families appropriately.
- Validation: compare the age pattern with NLSY79 child reports; do not force their levels to be equal because respondent and question formats differ.

### 6.5 Why age six cannot be identified from these items

The model starts child voice at age 6, but the most relevant CDS items begin at age 9 (`E45`) and age 12 (`E46`). `E43–E44` reach age 6 only in the 2019 and later designs, and they refer to room/clothing rather than study–leisure allocation.

Therefore:

- treat age 6 as an institutional/modeling assumption linked to school entry;
- do not claim that `E45–E46` empirically identify the onset;
- report robustness to voice beginning at ages 6, 9, 10, and 12;
- allow a smooth logistic age profile as a robustness check rather than only a linear profile.

### 6.6 A documentation issue to fix before presenting equations

The estimation memo uses $\tilde\mu_t=\mu_0+\mu_1(t-5)$ with $\mu_1=-0.04$, which makes the parent weight decline. The current `docs/model.txt` instead writes $1-\mu_1(t-5)$. With a negative $\mu_1$, that expression rises above one. The sign convention should be made consistent before the equation is shown to the supervisor.[^31][^32]

## 7. Practical construction and estimation checklist

### 7.1 CDS extraction

Extract:

- child identifiers, age, sex, wave, sample weight;
- `P14E43–P14E46`, `P19E43–P19E46`, `P21E43–P21E46`, `P24E43–P24E46`;
- relevant `C` rule items;
- caregiver identity, education, employment, family income, wealth, family structure, siblings;
- child study/leisure time and human-capital measures already used in the estimation.

Then:

1. convert `0`, `8`, `9`, and wave-specific nonresponses to labeled missing categories—not zero autonomy;
2. enforce each item's documented universe;
3. create a harmonized ages 12–17 sample for all four items;
4. also create domain-maximal samples: ages 9–17 for leisure and 12–17 for homework across all four waves;
5. tabulate weighted response shares by single year of age and wave;
6. inspect sample attrition and repeated-child counts;
7. test whether 2014 wording produces a discontinuity in `E45`.

### 7.2 NLSY79 extraction

In NLS Investigator, search by question text and select each survey year rather than relying only on mnemonic names. Extract:

- seven decision-maker items;
- four rule-existence, say, and argument blocks;
- parent communication/listening/knowledge items;
- child age in months, sex, birth order, height/maturity, cognitive tests, behavior, work, earnings, allowance;
- mother's education, employment, income, household structure;
- child weights and mother/child IDs.

Construct domain-by-domain indicators for child-only, child-plus-adult, and adult-only decisions. Preserve multi-response information. Cluster standard errors by mother and use sibling/mother fixed effects only where there is within-family variation.

### 7.3 Measurement diagnostics

- Report domain-specific results before any summary index.
- For an index, compare a simple count, exploratory factor model, and item-response model.
- Test differential item functioning by age, sex, wave, and respondent type.
- Treat shared decisions as a separate state, not necessarily the middle of an ordered scale.
- Compare caregiver and child reports where a dataset permits it; disagreement is data, not merely noise.
- Distinguish “granted autonomy” from rule-breaking, concealment, or resistance.
- Do not infer causality from age profiles alone: age, cohort, survey wave, and family changes are intertwined.

## 8. What I would propose to the supervisor

### Preferred proposal

> “I will retain PSID/CDS as the main U.S. dataset. I will construct domain-specific caregiver-reported autonomy measures from `E45` (leisure) and `E46` (homework) in 2014, 2019, 2021, and 2024. Initially I will use them to validate the assumed age profile of the child's welfare weight. In a second specification, I will add ordered-response autonomy moments to the SMM and estimate the welfare-weight slope while keeping the age slope of study productivity calibrated. I will use the NLSY79 Child Supplement's child-reported decision domains as external validation, particularly to show that sole and shared decision-making are different processes. I will not label any survey answer as the bargaining weight itself.”

### Why this is the best compromise

- It preserves the PSID family income, wealth, time, investment, and child-development structure already used in the thesis.
- It adds measurement directly related to the latent parameter instead of asking study time to identify both preferences and productivity.
- It uses the strongest U.S. child-report dataset without rebuilding the whole structural sample.
- It is honest about cardinal identification and avoids overclaiming.

### What not to propose yet

- Replacing PSID entirely with Add Health or FFCWS.
- Calibrating $\mu_t$ by mapping “very little/some/a lot” to 0/0.5/1.
- Calling a PCA score “bargaining power” without a measurement model.
- Estimating both $\mu_1$ and $\sigma_{41}$ with only the current ten moments.
- Treating realized child earnings as an exogenous bargaining shifter.

## 10. Likely supervisor questions and concise answers

**Q: Is there a direct bargaining-weight variable?**  
A: No. There are direct decision-authority questions, but a Pareto weight is latent and utility-normalization dependent.

**Q: Which two CDS variables are closest to your model?**  
A: `E45`, choice over leisure activities, and `E46`, choice over managing homework.

**Q: Why not average all four items?**  
A: Domains differ, age universes differ, and sole/shared authority need not be one-dimensional. I will report domain results first and validate any index psychometrically.

**Q: Why use NLSY79 if the model is estimated on PSID?**  
A: NLSY79 provides child-reported authority and a published economics precedent. I use it for external validation, not to pretend it is the same sample.

**Q: Can an autonomy response identify a numerical Pareto weight?**  
A: Only through an explicit measurement model and normalization, jointly with behavior. On its own, no.

**Q: Why not identify power from child study time?**  
A: Study time also depends on preferences, study productivity, human-capital returns, continuation values, and constraints. An autonomy item supplies a more targeted signal.

**Q: Can child earnings be a distribution factor?**  
A: Potential or policy-induced earnings may help, but realized earnings are endogenous to schooling, work, and the bargaining process.

**Q: Does “no rules” mean high autonomy?**  
A: Not necessarily; it can also mean weak monitoring. I would keep it separate rather than impose a monotone score.

**Q: Can CDS validate bargaining beginning at age 6?**  
A: Not for the study–leisure domains. It can discipline later growth; the onset must remain an assumption with sensitivity analyses.

**Q: What is the immediate next empirical output?**  
A: Weighted age-by-wave response shares for `E45/E46`, sample counts, and a comparison with the model-implied child-weight profile.
