\section{The Model}
\subsection{Environment}
The model starts with a 2-parents family at the birth of their child. It follows the parents until the child turns 18, and follows the child until his death. Parents are assumed to be age 26 at the start of the model when the child is born. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:life_cycle}, the child has 3 stages of life: childhood, adolescence, and adulthood. During the first two stages (until age 18), the child lives with the parents, after which the child separates and makes independent life-cycle decisions. After the child’s separation, parents’ life-cycle decisions are summarized using a terminal value rather than explicitly tracked. The model has no retirement stage and ends as the child becomes 68 years old. In the first stage of the child's life, periods $t = 1,\dots,5$ (child ages 1 to 5), all decisions related to the family and the child’s human capital formation are made by the parents. The human capital production function of the child is assumed to be in a Cobb–Douglas form, capturing dynamic complementary as the human capital at period $t + 1$ depends on human capital in period $t$. Parents annually decide on their consumption, labor supply, child's educational expenditure, and the time investment dedicated to the child. In the second stage, periods $t = 6,\dots,17$ (ages 6 to 17), as the child enters school, the child gradually gains autonomy within the family and becomes more and more involved in the decisions related to their human capital within a bargaining framework. Additionally, the child’s own study time is included in the human capital production function. We introduce a half-period between ages 17 and 18, during which parents transfer assets to the child, which become the child’s initial asset in the next stage. At the same time, parents and child jointly decide whether the child enrolls in college or enters the labor market. If the child attend the college, he faces consumption and saving decisions during college and enter the labor market at age 22. If the child work immediately, he face consumption, saving, and labor supply decisions from age 18. In both paths, the child remains in the labor market until age 68. The model prohibits borrowing, thereby ensuring that in each period, household expenditures exactly equal household income.

\subsection{The Childhood and Adolescence Problem}


\textbf{Preferences} \quad We consider a family consisting of the parents and a single child, who interact over a finite horizon $t = 1, \ldots, T_L-1$. The parents are assumed to act as a single decision-making unit. They derive utility from their own private consumption, leisure, and the human capital of their child. The private utility of the parents in period $t$ is defined as follows:

\begin{equation}
  U_{p,t}
 = \phi_{1}\frac{c_{p,t}^{1-\rho}}{1-\rho} + \phi_{2}\frac{l_{p,t}^{1-\eta}}{1-\eta} + \phi_{3}\,\ln HC_{t},
  \qquad l_{p,t} = 1 - h_{p,t} - \tau_{p,t},  \label{eq:parent-util}
\end{equation}

Here, $c_{p,t}$ denotes parental consumption, $l_{p,t}$ parental leisure, and $HC_t$ the child’s cognitive skills (or “ability”) measured at the start of period $t$. The preference weights $(\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3)$ are strictly positive. They are \emph{not} normalized to sum to one: consumption, leisure and the child's skill enter in different units, so the three are not commensurable and their sum has no interpretation. The weights are time-invariant. $\phi_1$ is normalised to one, and $\phi_2$ and $\phi_3$ are estimated by SMM; $\rho = 1.5$ and $\eta = 2$.

Note that parental leisure is $l_{p,t} = 1 - h_{p,t} - \tau_{p,t}$, so time spent with the child is priced at the same rate as time spent working. This matters: a specification that penalizes only $h_{p,t}$ makes $\tau_{p,t}$ a free good, and the parent then pushes it to the corner $h_{p,t} + \tau_{p,t} = 1$. The child’s period-$t$ utility is then specified as:

\begin{equation}
  U_{c,t}
  = \lambda_{1}\,\ln l_{c,t}
          + \lambda_{2}\,\ln HC_{t},
  \label{eq:child-util}
\end{equation}
where $l_{c,t}$ denotes the child’s leisure (play) time. The preference weights $(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})$ are strictly positive and normalized. 
To model the process of intra-household decision-making, we model the family’s joint utility consisting of parents and the child private utility with age-of-child varying welfare weights\footnote{\footnotesize{We do not explicitly model the process that generates these weights and take them as exogenous to the model.}}. the parents and the child bargain over the allocation of the child’s leisure and human capital investment once childhood ends. The parents’ bargaining weight is denoted by $\tilde{\mu}_t \in (0,1)$, while the child’s bargaining weight is $(1-\tilde{\mu}_t)$. During childhood ($t \leq 5$), the Pareto weight assigned to the child’s utility is set to zero, so all decisions are made by the parents. As the child enters adolescence, they gradually gain autonomy and more power within the family which translates to higher welfare weight in he family joint utility function. In addition, their own decision regarding the time investment in their human capital becomes relevant. The utility of the family is given by:
\begin{equation}
    U_{f,t} =
      \phi_1 \frac{C_t^{1-\rho}}{1-\rho}  
    + \phi_2 \frac{l_{p,t}^{1-\eta}}{1-\eta}
    + \tilde{\alpha}_1 \log l_{c,t}
    + \tilde{\alpha}_2 \log HC_t,
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{align}
    \tilde{\alpha}_1 &= (1 - \tilde{\mu}_t) \cdot \lambda_1, \label{eq:alpha1}\\
    \tilde{\alpha}_2 &= \tilde{\mu}_t \cdot \phi_3 + (1 - \tilde{\mu}_t) \cdot \lambda_2. \label{eq:alpha2}
\end{align}
Equations \eqref{eq:alpha1}–\eqref{eq:alpha2} define the effective utility weights on the child’s outcomes as a function of intra-household welfare weights. 
Specifically, $\tilde{\alpha}_1$ in \eqref{eq:alpha1} shows that the weight on the child’s leisure is entirely determined by the child’s welfare share $(1-\tilde{\mu}_t)$, while $\tilde{\alpha}_2$ in \eqref{eq:alpha2} illustrates that the weight on human capital reflects both parental valuation ($\phi_3$) and the child’s valuation ($\lambda_2$), combined according to the welfare weights. 


\noindent\textbf{Welfare Weights} \quad The Welfare weights are homogeneous across households and evolve over time according to equation below as the child ages.
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\mu}_{t} =
  \begin{cases}
    1,                 & \text{if } t \le 5,\\[4pt]
    1-\mu_{1}(t-5),    & \text{if } 5 < t \le 17,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:bargain-weight}
\end{equation}
This schedule implies that during childhood ($t \leq 5$), parents have full control ($\tilde{\mu}_t=1$), so the child’s utility carries no weight. From $t = 6$ (age 6) onward, as the child enters school and gradually gains autonomy, the parent’s weight decreases linearly while the child’s weight $(1-\tilde{\mu}_t)$ increases with age.

\noindent\textbf{Time and Budget Constraints} \quad The family’s choices in each period are subject to both budget and time constraints. The budget constraint determines the evolution of household assets:
\begin{equation}
    a_{t+1}
  = (1 + r)\,a_t
     + y - T(w_t\, h_t)
     + b_t - c_{p,t} - e_{p,t}, \qquad a_{t+1} \geq 0,
\end{equation}
where $a_{t}$ denotes household assets at the beginning of period $t$, $r$ is the interest rate, $w_t$ is the hourly wage rate, $h_{p,t}$ is parental labor supply \emph{per parent} --- household labor income is $2 \cdot \bar{w}_t \cdot h_{p,t}$, with $\bar{w}$ the mean parental wage --- $T(y)$ is the progressive taxation, $b_t$ represents non-labor income or transfers, $c_{p,t}$ is parental consumption, and $e_{p,t}$ is parental educational expenditure on the child. The non-negativity condition, $a_{t+1} \geq 0$, rules out borrowing. In order to model parental wages $w_t$, We collapse the mother and father into a single representative household, both age and wages are defined as the mean across the two parents. The wage equation is specified as: 

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\ln w_{it} = \beta_0
&+ \beta_1 \,\text{BothCollege}_i
+ \beta_2 \,\text{Age}_{it}
+ \beta_3 \,\text{Age}_{it}^2 \\
&+ \beta_4 \,(\text{BothCollege}_i \times \text{Age}_{it})
+ \beta_5 \,(\text{BothCollege}_i \times \text{Age}_{it}^2)
+ z_{it},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:wage_log}
\end{equation}

Average wage of household $i$ in period $t$ is denoted $w_{it}$, and $\text{BothCollege}_i$ is an indicator equal to one if both parents hold a college degree. $\text{Age}_{it}$ is measured in \emph{model time}, $=$ biological age $-\,25$, so period $1$ corresponds to parental age $26$; the estimation sample is re-indexed the same way. The quadratic age terms capture the hump-shaped life-cycle profile, while the interaction terms allow the slope and curvature to differ across education groups. The empirical estimates corresponding to Equation~\eqref{eq:wage_log} are reported in Table~\ref{tab:wage_estimates}. The error term $z_{it}$ represents a persistent stochastic shock. The estimated residual process is a permanent random walk plus a transitory component, with $\sigma_\iota = 0.189$ and $\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.134$. For tractability the model approximates it with a single \emph{stationary} AR(1) and omits the transitory component:
\begin{equation}
    z_{it} = \rho z_{i,t-1} + \varepsilon_{it},
    \qquad \varepsilon_{it} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
    \qquad \rho = 0.95, \quad \sigma = 0.2,
\end{equation}
discretized onto a finite state space by Rouwenhorst's method, which matches the unconditional variance and the first-order autocorrelation of the AR(1) exactly at any number of nodes (Kopecky and Suen, 2010); Tauchen's method overstates the unconditional standard deviation by 31\% at these parameters. The approximation is deliberate: $\rho < 1$ makes the shock stationary, so the transfer problem can integrate over a well-defined ergodic distribution.

%\label{eq:wage}
%\end{equation}
%where $w_0$ is the baseline wage, $\alpha$ governs the return to parental human capital and 

The time endowment in each period is normalized to one for both parent and child. For the parent, time is divided between labor supply ($h_{p,t}$), time spent on child care ($\tau_{p,t}$), and leisure ($l_{p,t}$):
\begin{equation}
  h_{p,t} + \tau_{p,t} + l_{p,t} = 1.  
\end{equation}
For the child, available time is allocated among study ($\tau_{c,t}$), time spent with the parent ($\tau_{p,t}$), and leisure ($l_{c,t}$):
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{c,t} + \tau_{p,t} + l_{c,t} = 1.
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Human Capital Production} \quad We use Cobb-Douglas specification for the child human capital production function. For childhood period $(t \leq 5)$, The child's human capital at $t+1$ is a function of child care time, parental expenditure, and the current level of child's human capital in period $t$. As soon as child enters Adolescence ($5 < t \leq 17$) the child own study time is also added to the production function. The production function is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \log HC_{t+1} =
\begin{cases}
\log R_t + \sigma_{1t} \log \tau_{p,t} + \sigma_{2t} \log e_{p,t} + \sigma_{3t} \log HC_t & \text{if } t \leq 5\\
\log R_t + \sigma_{1t} \log \tau_{p,t} + \sigma_{2t} \log e_{p,t} + \sigma_{3t} \log HC_t + \sigma_{4t} \log \tau_{c,t} & \text{if } 5< t \leq 17
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Here, $R_t$ is a total factor of productivity, $\tau_{p,t}$ is the time spent with the parent, $e_{p,t}$ is the expenditure on the child by the parents, $HC_t$ is the child’s cognitive ability at the start of period $t$, and $\tau_{c,t}$ is the child’s own study time. We use time-specific parameters because the productivity of different inputs changes by the child's age.

\subsubsection{Value Functions}
\label{sec:value-functions}

The family’s value function at period $t$ incorporates bargaining weights and private utilities into a unified dynamic decision-making framework. The state variables at the beginning of each period are defined as follows: $a_t$ denotes the household’s asset holdings, $k_t$ is the stock of parental human capital, and $HC_t$ represents the child’s cognitive ability. The value function can be specified over three distinct life stages:


\noindent\textbf{Childhood ($t \leq 5$)}: In this stage, child holds no exclusive welfare weight within the family, and as a result, the parents act as the single decision maker, choosing their consumption ($c_{p,t}$), educational investments in the child ($e_{p,t}$), labor supply ($h_{p,t}$), and parental time dedicated to child care ($\tau_{p,t}$).

    \begin{equation}
        V_t(a_t, k_t, HC_t) = \max_{c_{p,t},\,e_{p,t},\,h_{p,t},\,\tau_{p,t}}
        \left\{ U_{f,t} + \beta\, \mathbb{E}_{z}\left[ V_{t+1}(a_{t+1}, k_{t+1}, HC_{t+1})\right] \right\}
    \end{equation}
    
\noindent\textbf{Adolescence ($5 < t <17$)}: As the child transitions into adolescence, The child gains autonomy and gets involved in the intra-household decision making. The child’s choice regarding study time ($\tau_{c,t}$) becomes relevant. Accordingly, the family’s problem expands:
        \begin{equation}
        V_t(a_t, k_t, HC_t)=\max_{c_{p,t},\,e_{p,t},\,h_{p,t},\,\tau_{p,t},\,\tau_{c,t}}
        \left\{ U_{f,t} + \beta\, \mathbb{E}_{z}\left[ V_{t+1}(a_{t+1}, k_{t+1}, HC_{t+1})\right] \right\}
        \end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Last year of adolescence ($t = 17$)}: In the final decision-making period of the family, the family optimizes choices taking into account the uncertainty surrounding the child’s future adult outcomes. Formally, the value function incorporates an expectation over future shocks, where $z_0$ is an AR(1) wage shock that remains unresolved at this stage, and $\epsilon_0$ is a preference shock for the child’s college decision, realized at $t = T_L$. Here, $V_{T_L-1}^{\text{CD}}$ denotes the expected continuation value, which is defined in detail in Section~\ref{sec:terminal-period-utility} below.
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:T_L_1}
        V_t(a_t, k_t, HC_t) = \max_{c_{p,t},\,e_{p,t},\,h_{p,t},\,\tau_{p,t},\,\tau_{c,t}}
        \left\{ U_{f,t} + \beta\, \mathbb{E}_{z_0, \epsilon_0}\left[ V_{T_L-1}^{\text{CD}}(a_{t+1}, HC_{t+1}) \right] \right\}
    \end{equation}


\subsubsection{Transfer and College Decision}
\label{sec:terminal-period-utility}


In the start of terminal period ($t = T_L$), the parent and child cooperatively decide whether the child should enroll in college or enter the workforce. This joint decision is made through a collective decision making framework, with welfare weight $\theta \in (0,1)$ assigned to the parent’s utility and $(1-\theta)$ to the child’s utility. Conditional on this decision, the parent chooses the level of altruistic transfer, $tr$, to the child. This choice is made after all the uncertainties in the period are realized. 


In addition to the baseline case with homogeneous beliefs, we consider a more general framework with heterogeneous beliefs across families. Each agent holds a belief $m$ with a subjective estimate $\beta_E^m$ of the log college wage premium, elicited as a believed ratio of college to high-school earnings and anchored to the career average of the premium profile. These beliefs influence both the family’s investment in the child’s human capital during childhood and adolescence, and the size of the transfer allocated for college. Consequently, they also shape the joint decision regarding college enrollment and the perceived post‐graduation wage profile. Upon graduation, agents observe the \emph{true} premium $\beta_E^\star$ and update their beliefs accordingly. Because human capital no longer accumulates in adulthood, there is no perceived stock that can drift from the true one over the college years, so no reconciliation term is required: the truth arrives as a one-time surprise at labour-market entry.

The parent's utility in this period, incorporates altruism toward the child, direct utility from their own terminal assets, and prestige toward child's current stock of human capital. The parent's utility is given by:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    V_{T_L}^P &= \psi_{\mathrm{term}} \log HC_{T_L}
    + \kappa_{\mathrm{term}} \log a_{\mathrm{term}}
    + \omega V_{T_L}^C \\
    &\text{s.t.} \quad a_{\mathrm{term}} = a_{T_L} - tr.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\psi_{\mathrm{term}}$ denotes the parent’s weight on the child’s human capital, which may reflect paternalism or concern for prestige; $\kappa_{\mathrm{term}}$ captures utility from terminal assets\footnote{Since we do not model parental choices after the child leaves the household, $\kappa_{\mathrm{term}}$ represents the value of assets for late-life consumption, retirement, and potential monetary transfers to the child.}; and $\omega$ is the weight on parental altruism. Here, $HC_{T_L}$ is the child’s human capital, $a_{\mathrm{term}}$ denotes the parent’s terminal asset holdings for later life, and $V_{T_L}^C$ is the child’s continuation value.

The child’s terminal value reflects a discrete choice between college ($E$) and work ($W$), based on the realized preference shock for college:
\begin{equation}
    V_{T_L}^C(tr, HC_{T_L}, \epsilon_0, z_{T_L}, m) =
    \max \left\{
        V_0^E(tr, HC_{T_L}, \epsilon_0, z_{T_L}, m),
        \;
        V_0^W(tr, HC_{T_L}, z_{T_L})
    \right\}
\end{equation}

In the separation period at $T_L$, the family chooses the transfer to the child by solving:
\begin{equation}
    V_{T_L}^{\mathrm{CD}}(a_{T_L}, HC_{T_L}, \epsilon_0, m) =
\max_{0 \leq tr \leq a_{T_L} - \bar{a}^P} \left\{
\mathbb{E}_{z_{T_L}} \left[
(1 - \theta) V_{T_L}^C(tr, HC_{T_L}, \epsilon_0, z_{T_L}, m)
+ \theta V_{T_L}^P(tr, HC_{T_L}, \epsilon_0, m)
\right]
\right\}
\end{equation}
where $V_{T_L}^C$ and $V_{T_L}^P$ are the child’s and parent’s value functions, respectively, as functions of the transfer $tr$ and the college/work choice $d$. $\bar{a}^P$ is the minimum asset the parent retains after transferring, set to one period's minimum consumption; without it the parent's terminal value $\kappa \log(a_{T_L} - tr)$ diverges at $tr = a_{T_L}$. At period $T_L-1$ the preference shock has not yet been realized, so the value function takes the expectation over $\epsilon_0$ of the period-$T_L$ value --- it does \emph{not} re-optimize:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:T_L_1}
    V_{T_L-1}^{\mathrm{CD}}(a_{T_L}, HC_{T_L}, m) =
        \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon_0} \left[ V_{T_L}^{\mathrm{CD}}(a_{T_L}, HC_{T_L}, \epsilon_0, m) \right]
\end{equation}
The nesting is the substance. Writing $\max_{tr} \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon_0,\, z_{T_L}}[\cdot]$ instead would place the maximum \emph{outside} the $\epsilon_0$ expectation, which is a commitment timing: the family would have to fix the transfer before seeing the preference shock, and the $\epsilon_0$ expectation would be taken twice. The timing above is
$\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon_0}\!\left[\max_{d,\, tr} \mathbb{E}_{z_{T_L}}[\cdot]\right]$: $\epsilon_0$ is observed at the half period, enrollment and the transfer condition on it, and only $z_{T_L}$ remains unresolved when they are chosen. This is what the code implements.

The optimal enrollment and transfer choice are:
\begin{equation}
    d^*(a_{T_L}, HC_{T_L}, \epsilon_0) =
     \arg\max_{0 \leq tr \leq a_{T_L} - \bar{a}^P}\left\{
            \mathbb{E}_{z_{T_L}} \left[
                (1-\theta)\, V_{T_L}^C(tr, HC_{T_L}, \epsilon_0, z_{T_L}, m)
                + \theta\, V_{T_L}^P(tr, a_{T_L}, HC_{T_L}, \epsilon_0, m)
            \right]
        \right\}
\end{equation}





\subsection{Adulthood Problem}
\label{sec:adulthood}

Following the joint decision taken at the half period ($t=T_L$), the child leaves the
household and solves an individual life-cycle problem. It is convenient to re-index time
from the child's point of view: let $s=1,\dots,T_D$ denote the periods of adulthood, with
$s=1$ corresponding to biological age $18$ and, in general,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{age}(s) = 17 + s, \qquad s = 1,\dots,T_D,
  \label{eq:age-index}
\end{equation}
so that $T_D = 51$ periods carry the individual from age $18$ to age $68$, at which point
life ends. There is no retirement stage: the individual either studies or works in every
period. Enrollment lasts $T_E = 4$ periods, $s = 1,\dots,4$ (ages $18$ to $21$); a graduate
enters the labor market at $s = T_E+1$, that is at age $22$.\footnote{The four-year college
and the age-$22$ entry are properties of the solved model, not of the display: the college
problem is solved on $s=1,\dots,4$ and the graduate's working life on $s=5,\dots,51$.}

\noindent\textbf{Inheritance at separation} \quad
The individual enters adulthood with four objects fixed at the half period. The parental
transfer becomes initial wealth, $a_1 = tr$. The stock of cognitive skill accumulated
during childhood and adolescence is inherited as
\begin{equation}
  HC \equiv HC_{T_L},
  \label{eq:theta-handoff}
\end{equation}
and, as explained below, remains \emph{constant for the rest of life}.\footnote{The
implementation and the model documentation write this object as $\theta$. We keep the
thesis's own symbol $HC$ for the child's cognitive skill, because $\theta$ already denotes
the parent's welfare weight in the college--work decision of
Section~\ref{sec:terminal-period-utility}, and no symbol should carry two meanings.
Parameters indexed to skill are written with the subscript $\mathrm{HC}$ for the same
reason.} The household type
$\text{BothCollege}\in\{0,1\}$ is carried over from the family stage and enters only the
psychic cost of enrollment. Finally, the persistent wage shock $z_1$ is drawn from the
\emph{stationary} distribution of the process in \eqref{eq:z-adult}, which is the same
distribution the transfer problem of Section~\ref{sec:terminal-period-utility} integrates
over. We write $E\in\{0,1\}$ for the education indicator, equal to one for a college
graduate and zero otherwise; $E$ is determined by the discrete choice $d^*$ at $T_L$ and is
thereafter a fixed characteristic.

The state vector in adulthood is therefore $(a_s, z_s)$, with $(HC, E,
\text{BothCollege})$ fixed characteristics rather than state variables that evolve. In the
work phase the individual chooses consumption and hours, $(c_s, h_s)$; during college the
only choice is consumption, since students do not supply labor, $h_s \equiv 0$.

\noindent\textbf{Preferences} \quad
A worker's period utility is defined over consumption and hours,
\begin{equation}
U^W(c_s,h_s) = \frac{c_s^{1-\rho}}{1-\rho} - \varphi\,\frac{h_s^{1+\eta}}{1+\eta},
\qquad
U^W(c_s,h_s)\big|_{\rho=1} = \ln c_s - \varphi\,\frac{h_s^{1+\eta}}{1+\eta},
\label{eq:util-work}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the coefficient of relative risk aversion, $\eta$ governs the Frisch
elasticity of labor supply, and $\varphi$ scales the disutility of work. The logarithmic
case is the limit $\rho \to 1$ and is implemented explicitly, so $\rho = 1$ is admissible;
the baseline uses $\rho = 1.5$. A student's period utility replaces the disutility of work
with the psychic cost of enrollment defined in \eqref{eq:psychic},
\begin{equation}
U^E(c_s;HC) = \frac{c_s^{1-\rho}}{1-\rho} - \kappa_X(HC),
\label{eq:util-college}
\end{equation}
with the same logarithmic case at $\rho = 1$.

\noindent\textbf{Human capital after childhood} \quad
Cognitive skill does not accumulate in adulthood:
\begin{equation}
  HC_{s+1} = HC_s = HC, \qquad s = 1,\dots,T_D .
  \label{eq:hc-constant}
\end{equation}
This replaces the learning-by-doing law of motion of earlier versions of the model, in
which skill grew with hours worked and received a fixed increment in each college year.
Under \eqref{eq:hc-constant} the three distinct returns that a single stock previously had
to carry---the return to childhood investment, the return to college, and the return to
labor-market experience---are separated and priced independently in the wage equation
below. Childhood skill $HC$ is rewarded through its own elasticity, college through the
education indicator $E$, and experience through an education-specific age profile.

\noindent\textbf{Wage determination} \quad
The log hourly wage is linear in log childhood skill, in the education indicator, and in a
quadratic in biological age, with education-specific slopes throughout:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\ln w_s
&= \ln w_0 \;+\; \beta_E\,E
 \;+\; \bigl(\alpha_{\mathrm{HC}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{HC}E}\,E\bigr)\bigl(\ln HC - m_{\mathrm{HC}}\bigr) \\[2pt]
&\quad
 +\; \bigl(\gamma_1 + \gamma_{1E}\,E\bigr)\,\mathrm{age}(s)
 \;+\; \bigl(\gamma_2 + \gamma_{2E}\,E\bigr)\,\mathrm{age}(s)^2
 \;+\; \ln z_s \;+\; \ln \Omega .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:wage-adult}
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha_{\mathrm{HC}}$ is the \emph{elasticity} of the wage with respect to childhood human
capital for a high-school worker and $\alpha_{\mathrm{HC}} + \alpha_{\mathrm{HC}E}$ the corresponding
elasticity for a graduate; $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ trace the hump-shaped age profile of a
high-school worker and $\gamma_{1E},\gamma_{2E}$ the additional curvature enjoyed by
graduates. Because the interaction terms enter the age polynomial, the college premium is
not a single number but a profile that widens over the career.

Two constants in \eqref{eq:wage-adult} carry no behavior. The centering constant $m_{\mathrm{HC}}$
is set near the mean of $\ln HC$ produced by the family stage, so that $\ln w_0$ reads as
the log wage of an individual of average childhood skill; any change in $m_{\mathrm{HC}}$ is offset
exactly by $\ln w_0$. The scaling constant $\Omega$ converts the estimated hourly profile
into the annual units in which the model's time endowment is normalized. Both are
normalizations rather than estimated parameters, and neither affects any
choice.\footnote{$\alpha_{\mathrm{HC}}$ is an elasticity and deliberately not a per-standard-deviation
effect. Standardizing $\ln HC$ by its simulated dispersion makes the marginal return to
childhood investment depend on the dispersion the model itself generates, which creates a
fixed-point problem in the calibration and, in practice, unbounded parental investment.}

The persistent component is a stationary AR(1) in logs,
\begin{equation}
  \ln z_s = \rho_z \ln z_{s-1} + \varepsilon^z_s,
  \qquad \varepsilon^z_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_z^2),
  \qquad |\rho_z| < 1,
  \label{eq:z-adult}
\end{equation}
with $\ln z_1$ drawn from the stationary distribution.\footnote{We write $\rho_z$ for the
persistence of the wage shock to keep it distinct from the coefficient of relative risk
aversion $\rho$ in \eqref{eq:util-work}; earlier drafts used $\rho$ for both. Stationarity
($\rho_z<1$) is what makes the ergodic distribution in \eqref{eq:theta-handoff}'s companion
draw well defined, and is a deliberate approximation to the permanent-plus-transitory
process estimated on the data.}

\noindent\textbf{Taxes, transfers and budget constraints} \quad
Labor income is taxed by the same progressive schedule as in the family stage: gross labor
income $w_s h_s$ yields net income
\begin{equation}
  T\!\left(w_s h_s\right) \;=\; \lambda\,(w_s h_s)^{1-\tau},
  \label{eq:tax-adult}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ governs progressivity and $\lambda$ the level. Every individual also receives
the government benefit $b$ in each period of adulthood. A worker's assets then evolve as
\begin{equation}
  a_{s+1} = (1+r)\,a_s + \lambda\,(w_s h_s)^{1-\tau} + b - c_s,
  \qquad a_{s+1} \ge 0 ,
  \label{eq:budget-work}
\end{equation}
and a student's as
\begin{equation}
  a_{s+1} = (1+r)\,a_s - c_s - c_{\text{college}} + b ,
  \qquad a_{s+1} \ge \underline{a}_{s+1} ,
  \label{eq:budget-college}
\end{equation}
where $c_{\text{college}}$ is the direct annual cost of attendance. Borrowing is prohibited
throughout adulthood, exactly as in the family stage.

The bound $\underline{a}_{s+1}$ in \eqref{eq:budget-college} is stronger than
non-negativity, and the difference is economic rather than numerical. Because students
cannot borrow and cannot work, a student must hold enough wealth at every point to fund the
\emph{remaining} years of college. Let $\underline{c}$ denote a minimum consumption level.
The requirement is the backward recursion
\begin{equation}
  \underline{a}_{T_E+1} = 0,
  \qquad
  \underline{a}_{s} = \frac{\underline{a}_{s+1} + \underline{c} + c_{\text{college}} - b}{1+r},
  \qquad s = T_E,\dots,1 ,
  \label{eq:areq}
\end{equation}
so that $\underline{a}_1$ is the wealth needed at age $18$ to complete a degree. Enrollment
is infeasible for an individual who would receive less than $\underline{a}_1$, and this
feasibility threshold---not the psychic cost---is what excludes the poorest households from
college in the model.

\noindent\textbf{The psychic cost of college} \quad
Each enrolled year carries a utility cost that falls with the child's own ability and with
parental education:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_X(HC) = \kappa_0 + \kappa_{\mathrm{HC}} \ln HC + \kappa_{\mathrm{ParEd}}\,\text{BothCollege},
  \qquad \kappa_{\mathrm{HC}} < 0, \quad \kappa_{\mathrm{ParEd}} < 0 .
  \label{eq:psychic}
\end{equation}
The log-linear form and the signs follow \citet{Colas2021} and \citet{Daruich2024}: abler
children, and children of college-educated parents, find enrollment less costly. This
replaces the earlier reciprocal-power specification $\kappa/(HC+1)^{2}$, which had no
counterpart in either paper and no parental-education term.

Because $HC$ is constant over the college years and the parental-education term does not
interact with consumption, the $\kappa_{\mathrm{ParEd}}$ component is additively separable
and constant across enrolled periods. It therefore shifts the value of the college path by
the closed-form annuity $-\kappa_{\mathrm{ParEd}}\,\text{BothCollege}\sum_{j=0}^{T_E-1}\beta^{j}$
and leaves every college policy function unchanged. This is an exact restatement, not an
approximation, and it means parental education operates on the enrollment margin without
adding a state variable.

\noindent\textbf{The preference shock and its timing} \quad
The taste shock $\epsilon_0$ is drawn once, at the half period $T_L$, and is observed by the
family \emph{before} the enrollment and transfer decisions are taken, which is what makes
those decisions condition on it. It is a shock to the value of \emph{attending college}
only: it enters the student's first period and has no counterpart on the work path. It is
neither persistent nor redrawn---there is a single realization, valued once at $s=1$. We
take $\epsilon_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_\epsilon^2)$.

\subsubsection{Work Path}

An individual in the labor market---whether a high-school worker from age $18$ or a graduate
from age $22$---solves a standard consumption, saving and labor-supply problem. Both
education groups solve the \emph{same} problem, but at different wages: $E$ enters
\eqref{eq:wage-adult} through the intercept, the skill elasticity and the age profile, so
the two working lives are genuinely distinct and are solved separately.\footnote{In earlier
versions the graduate's working life was copied from the high-school solution, so that a
graduate and a high-school worker holding the same stock faced an identical wage and college
paid only through the stock increment it added. That increment no longer exists, so the two
problems must be, and are, solved independently.} The Bellman equation is
\begin{equation}
V^W_s(a_s, z_s; HC, E) = \max_{c_s,\,h_s}
\left\{ U^W(c_s,h_s)
+ \beta\, \mathbb{E}_{z_{s+1}\mid z_s} \bigl[ V^W_{s+1}(a_{s+1}, z_{s+1}; HC, E) \bigr]
\right\},
\label{eq:bellman-work}
\end{equation}
subject to \eqref{eq:wage-adult}, \eqref{eq:budget-work} and $h_s \in [0,1]$, with $HC$
fixed by \eqref{eq:hc-constant}. The terminal condition is that the individual works, holds
no bequest motive, and consumes the entire budget in the last period of life:
\begin{equation}
V^W_{T_D}(a_{T_D}, z_{T_D}; HC, E) = \max_{h_{T_D}}
\; U^W\!\bigl(a_{T_D} + \lambda (w_{T_D} h_{T_D})^{1-\tau} + b,\; h_{T_D}\bigr),
\qquad V^W_{T_D+1} \equiv 0 .
\label{eq:terminal-adult}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{College Path}

A student consumes and saves out of the transfer and the benefit, pays the cost of
attendance, and supplies no labor. For $s = 1,\dots,T_E$ the value of enrollment is
\begin{equation}
V^E_s(a_s, z_s; HC, \epsilon_0) =
\max_{c_s}
\left\{ U^E(c_s;HC) + \mathbf{1}\{s=1\}\,\epsilon_0
      + \beta\, \mathbb{E}_{z_{s+1}\mid z_s}\bigl[ \mathcal{V}_{s+1} \bigr] \right\},
\label{eq:bellman-college}
\end{equation}
subject to \eqref{eq:budget-college} and \eqref{eq:areq}, where the continuation value
switches to the graduate's working life in the final college year:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{V}_{s+1} =
\begin{cases}
V^E_{s+1}(a_{s+1}, z_{s+1}; HC, \epsilon_0), & s < T_E, \\[4pt]
V^W_{s+1}(a_{s+1}, z_{s+1}; HC, E=1), & s = T_E .
\end{cases}
\label{eq:college-continuation}
\end{equation}
Graduation is therefore not an event in the law of motion of any stock: it is the point at
which the individual begins to draw wages from the $E=1$ profile in \eqref{eq:wage-adult}.
There is no human-capital increment from college, and consequently no reconciliation of a
perceived stock with a true one at graduation---the object earlier drafts corrected with the
term $\widetilde{HC}_{T_E} + b^{\star} + (T_E-1)(b^{\star}-b_m)$ has no counterpart in the
current model.

\noindent\textbf{Subjective beliefs about the return to college} \quad
Under the baseline all families know the true wage process. In the subjective-expectations
environment, a family of belief type $m$ misperceives \emph{one} object: the level of the
college wage premium. Beliefs are elicited as a relative college earnings ratio $rce_m$---a
single number for how much more a graduate earns over a career---and mapped into the model's
premium by anchoring on the career average of the estimated premium profile:
\begin{equation}
  \beta_E^{m}
  = \ln(rce_m) \;-\; \frac{1}{T_D - T_E}\sum_{s=T_E+1}^{T_D}
      \Bigl[\gamma_{1E}\,\mathrm{age}(s) + \gamma_{2E}\,\mathrm{age}(s)^2\Bigr].
  \label{eq:belief-map}
\end{equation}
The true intercept cancels in \eqref{eq:belief-map}, so the mapping depends only on the
education interactions: a believer's premium profile is the estimated profile shifted in
parallel. Beliefs move the \emph{level} of the premium and leave its \emph{shape} at the
estimated one. The homogeneous-belief baseline is the special case $\beta_E^m = \beta_E$.

Beliefs matter for choices in exactly two places. First, they change the perceived value of
the college path in \eqref{eq:bellman-college}, and therefore both the enrollment decision
$d^*$ and the size of the transfer chosen at $T_L$; because the family anticipates that
decision, beliefs also feed back into human-capital investment during childhood and
adolescence. Second, they govern consumption and saving during the four college years, which
are taken against the perceived graduate wage. On graduation the true premium is revealed:
the individual re-optimizes against the true wage process from age $22$ onward, and realized
wages, income and assets are always generated by the true $\beta_E$. An optimistic family
therefore over-saves relative to what its child will earn and receives a downward surprise at
labor-market entry; a pessimistic family receives an upward one.

This replaces the earlier formulation in which beliefs concerned $b_m$, a subjective
human-capital increment per year of college. That object no longer exists, and the belief is
now stated in the units the underlying survey evidence actually measures: \citet{Bleemer2018}
elicit beliefs about \emph{earnings} returns, not about a skill stock.
